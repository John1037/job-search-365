# How to Handle Rejection and Stay Motivated

Rejection — and its more common cousin, silence — is a normal, expected part of almost every job search, not a sign of doing something wrong. Most searches involve dozens of "no"s (or non-responses) before an offer, purely as a function of the numbers involved, not as a verdict on any individual candidate.

## Reframing rejection

- A rejection reflects one employer's specific needs at one specific moment — team fit, budget, an internal candidate, timing — far more often than it reflects a candidate's overall worth or ability. Most of the reasons behind a "no" are invisible to the applicant and often have nothing to do with them.
- It's genuinely fine to feel disappointed, especially after investing real time and hope in a role. Acknowledging that briefly, rather than suppressing it, tends to help people move past it faster than pretending it doesn't sting.
- Where feedback is offered, it's worth taking seriously; where it isn't (which is common — many employers don't give any), it's not worth over-interpreting the silence into a specific story about what went wrong.

## Staying motivated over a long search

- **Track activity, not just outcomes.** A sent application, a reply, a scheduled call — these are all forward progress, even when the final answer is no. Momentum from small, visible steps is what sustains motivation over months, not waiting for the one big yes.
- **Set a weekly rhythm rather than an open-ended goal** — a target number of applications and networking touches per week is easier to sustain and easier to judge progress against than "get a job" as the only measure of a good week.
- **Expect it to take a while.** Job searches commonly run several months, and it's entirely normal for a search several months in to still be ongoing — that's on schedule for a large share of job seekers, not behind.
- **Build in real breaks.** Constant searching without rest tends to produce worse applications, not more offers — treating the search itself sustainably tends to outperform treating it as a sprint.
- **Get support from people going through the same thing**, or who've been through it before — isolation tends to make rejection feel heavier than it needs to.

## When it's worth reassessing, not just persisting

If a specific type of rejection repeats consistently — always at the same stage, always for the same stated reason — that's worth treating as real signal and adjusting (the CV, the target roles, the interview approach), rather than only pushing through with more of the same.
